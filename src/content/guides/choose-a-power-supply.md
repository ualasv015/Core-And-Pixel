The power supply unit (**PSU**) converts wall power into the voltages the PC's components use. Choosing one means checking the whole system, not adding a graphics-card number to a CPU number and buying the first PSU with a slightly higher wattage.

## Start with the exact graphics card

Graphics cards vary in power use, connectors and manufacturer recommendations. Check the specifications for the exact card model you plan to buy. Partner cards based on the same GPU can differ in power connectors and recommended system power, so do not rely only on a reference model's figures.

Then check the processor's power specifications, plus the rest of the build: motherboard, storage, fans, cooling and any planned upgrades. A graphics-card manufacturer's recommended system power is a useful starting point, but the right choice still depends on the complete system and PSU quality.

## Check connectors and the case

Confirm that the PSU has the connectors required by the motherboard, CPU and graphics card. Follow the component and PSU manuals for cable connections. Do not force a connector or assume a cable from another modular PSU is compatible; manufacturers may use different wiring even when the plug looks similar.

Check the PSU's physical size and the case's supported form factor. Make sure there is room for the PSU, its cables and airflow. A unit that meets the wattage target but does not fit the case or lacks the correct connectors is not suitable.

## Consider quality, protections and noise

Wattage does not describe build quality, voltage regulation, protections, noise or warranty. Look for a reputable manufacturer, clear specifications, appropriate safety protections and independent technical reviews of the exact model. An efficiency certification describes conversion efficiency at specified loads; it is not by itself a complete quality rating.

Leave reasonable capacity for the planned workload and transient demand, but do not buy an unnecessarily oversized unit based on a generic online calculator. If you are unsure, compare the component manufacturers' recommendations and ask the PSU maker to confirm the specific configuration.

## Understand efficiency labels and newer connectors

An 80 PLUS label describes efficiency at specified loads and under a certification programme. It does not independently prove that a unit has strong voltage regulation, low ripple, quiet cooling or the protections you need. Read an independent technical review of the exact PSU model and revision when available; similarly named units can use different internal designs.

New graphics cards may use a high-power connector supplied by the card maker, while other models use standard PCIe 8-pin cables. Check the exact cable and PSU standard required by the card. Modular cables are not interchangeable across PSU brands or even across product families unless the PSU maker confirms the pinout.

## Think about the whole build and its use

Power needs change with the graphics card, CPU, overclocking settings, number of drives and sustained workload. A gaming system may draw less than a long render or combined CPU-and-GPU stress test. Leave headroom for normal load changes, but size it from the actual component list and PSU guidance instead of adding an arbitrary percentage to a calculator result.

If you plan to keep a PSU through several GPU upgrades, consider its connector set, warranty, protections and future system power needs. Do not keep a unit that shows damage, unstable behaviour or cable overheating just because its label has a high wattage.

## A practical PSU checklist

1. Identify the exact CPU and graphics-card models.
2. Check the graphics-card maker's recommended system power and connector requirements.
3. Confirm the PSU's output capacity, connectors and case dimensions.
4. Check the PSU model's independent reviews, protections, warranty and noise behaviour.
5. Follow the manufacturer's cable instructions and verify every connector is fully seated.
6. Recheck the plan if you change the CPU, GPU or overclocking settings.

Buy the power supply for the exact system you are building. There is no single wattage that is right for every PC with the same graphics-card family.
